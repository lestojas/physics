REFERENCE BASELINE — WHAT THE SLIDES ACTUALLY COVER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The slide deck derives and applies FOUR kinematic equations for HORIZONTAL,
one-dimensional, constant-acceleration motion only:
  Eq. 1:  v  = v0 + at                    (no x)
  Eq. 2:  x  = x0 + v0 t + (1/2) a t^2     (no v)
  Eq. 3:  v^2 = v0^2 + 2a(x - x0)          (no t)
  Eq. 4:  x  = x0 + (1/2)(v0 + v) t        (no a)
  plus the intermediate relation  v_avg = (v0 + v) / 2  (valid ONLY for
  constant acceleration).
Four guided examples model the expected problem style: single-object,
one or two "phases" at most, direct algebraic substitution/rearrangement,
careful sign convention (e.g., negative acceleration = deceleration).
The slides do NOT cover: free fall / vertical motion with g, projectile
motion, two-object relative motion or pursuit problems, graphical (x-t,
v-t, a-t) analysis/sketching, or calculus (derivatives/integrals) for
non-constant acceleration.

CLASSIFICATION CRITERIA USED BELOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EASY          - Single-phase, single (horizontal) object; solved by direct
                substitution into one (or a simple immediate two-step chain
                of) the four equations, same structure/method as the four
                Guided Examples or the explicit vavg=(v0+v)/2 relation.
INTERMEDIATE  - Still only the four equations / vavg relation and still
                horizontal/single "object system," BUT requires multiple
                phases of motion, an intermediate unknown, quadratic
                root handling, or symbolic derivation - i.e., a multi-step
                approach not directly modeled by a single Guided Example.
CHALLENGING   - Requires a concept or technique NOT covered on the slides:
                free fall / vertical motion (uses g), two independently
                moving objects (relative motion, pursuit, collision),
                graph reading or sketching as the core task, calculus
                (explicit CALC problems or non-constant acceleration),
                or experimental/DATA curve-fitting.

Each entry below lists the item number, a short description (NOT solved),
and the one-line justification for its category. Page references are to
the printed textbook page numbers appearing in the PDF footer.

EASY  (12 items)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Q2.1 - Does a car's speedometer measure speed or velocity? Explain.
   [Direct conceptual check on velocity vs. sign/direction, mirrors slides'
   emphasis on sign conventions (Learning Objective 3).]

2. Q2.12 - Can a car have velocity toward the west and acceleration toward
   the east at the same time? Under what circumstances?
   [Directly matches the slides' negative-acceleration/deceleration concept
   used in Guided Example 1 (parcel of air, a = -4.00 m/s^2) and slide 8's
   "car braking" bullet.]

3. Q2.14 - Under constant acceleration, avg. velocity is half the sum of
   initial and final velocities. Is this still true if acceleration is not
   constant? Explain.
   [This is essentially the slide's own vavg=(v0+v)/2 caveat, stated almost
   verbatim in the slide 6 notes ("valid only because acceleration is
   constant").]

4. Exercise 2.1 (p.59) - Car travels +x at avg. velocity 6.25 m/s for 4.00 s;
   find distance traveled.
   [Single direct use of x = x0 + v_avg*t, the same sub-equation used in
   slide 13's derivation of Equation 4.]

5. Exercise 2.3 (p.59) - "Trip Home": compare travel times for two different
   average speeds over the same distance.
   [Single-step rearrangement of distance = speed x time, same algebraic
   level as the Guided Examples.]

6. Exercise 2.16 (p.60) - Astronaut's velocity changes over a 10 s interval;
   find magnitude, sign, and direction of average acceleration for three
   cases.
   [Directly a = (v-v0)/t, the exact defining equation used to derive
   Equation 1 in slide 10; also reinforces sign-convention learning
   objective.]

7. Exercise 2.19 (p.60) - Antelope covers 70.0 m in 6.00 s with constant
   acceleration; final speed 15.0 m/s. Find initial speed and acceleration.
   [Same structure as Guided Example 3/4: combine Eq.4 (or vavg) with Eq.1,
   single horizontal phase.]

8. Exercise 2.20 (p.60) - Tennis serve: ball leaves racquet at 73.14 m/s
   after 30.0 ms constant acceleration from rest. Find acceleration and
   distance traveled.
   [Direct v=v0+at then x from average-velocity relation - identical
   method to Guided Example 3.]

9. Exercise 2.21 (p.60) - Fastest pitch: ball reaches 45.0 m/s over 1.50 m
   with constant acceleration. Find acceleration and time.
   [Direct application of v^2=v0^2+2a(x-x0) then v=v0+at - identical
   method to Guided Example 4.]

10. Exercise 2.23 (p.60) - BIO Airbags: max survivable acceleration is
    250 m/s^2; initial speed 105 km/h. Find minimum stopping distance.
    [Single direct solve of v^2=v0^2+2a(x-x0) for distance.]

11. Exercise 2.25 (p.60) - BIO Airbag injuries: constant acceleration 60g
    for 36 ms; find stopping distance.
    [Two direct chained steps (find v0 implied by a & t, then x) - same
    length/style as a Guided Example.]

12. MCAT Passage Problem 2.90 (p.65) - Contraction of the heart's left
    ventricle lasts 250 ms; blood speed 0.80 m/s at end of contraction
    (starts from rest). Find average acceleration.
    [Direct a=(v-v0)/t, identical in form to Exercise 2.16 and the
    Equation-1 derivation basis.]

INTERMEDIATE  (20 items)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Q2.3 - Can an object with constant acceleration reverse its direction of
   travel? Can it reverse direction twice? Explain reasoning.
   [Requires deeper reasoning about the linear nature of v(t)=v0+at beyond
   the single sign-flip shown in Guided Example 1.]

2. Q2.4 - Under what conditions is average velocity equal to instantaneous
   velocity?
   [Conceptual extension of vavg beyond the constant-acceleration midpoint
   case explicitly given on the slides.]

3. Q2.6 - Under what conditions does the magnitude of average velocity equal
   average speed?
   [Requires reasoning about direction consistency, not explicitly on the
   slides but solvable using only slide-level concepts.]

4. Q2.7 - Comparative reasoning about two cars' average velocities between
   two crossing events (Dodge Viper / BMW puzzle).
   [Requires relational algebraic reasoning about average velocity over the
   same interval; no free fall/graphs/calculus needed.]

5. Exercise 2.2 (p.59) - Shearwater seabird flown 5150 km, returns to nest in
   13.5 days; find average velocity for (a) return flight and (b) whole
   round-trip episode.
   [Two-part, requires recognizing that net displacement for the round trip
   is different from one-way — an extra reasoning step beyond direct
   substitution.]

6. Exercise 2.4 (p.59) - "Pillar to Post": 200 m east at 5.0 m/s then 280 m
   west at 4.0 m/s; find average speed and average velocity.
   [Two-segment motion, opposite directions — requires combining two
   distance/time relations, more than a single substitution.]

7. Exercise 2.5 (p.59) - Walk 60.0 m east then 40.0 m west with different
   times for each leg; find average velocity and average speed for entire
   trip.
   [Two-segment displacement/time problem, same intermediate complexity as
   Exercise 2.4.]

8. Exercise 2.18 (p.60) - Estimate freeway on-ramp distance for a car
   accelerating 30 mph to 70 mph; find the average acceleration.
   [Open-ended estimation requiring the student to set up and use the UAM
   equations, an extra layer beyond direct plug-in.]

9. Exercise 2.22 (p.60) - Highway braking from 70 mph to 30 mph: estimate
   time, find average acceleration, and extend the analysis to a full stop
   and total stopping distance.
   [Multi-part with an estimation step plus a second calculation building
   on the first result.]

10. Exercise 2.24 (p.60) - Car enters freeway ramp from rest, reaches 20 m/s
    over 120 m; find acceleration and time; then relate to freeway traffic
    moving at constant 20 m/s during that same time.
    [Parts (a)-(b) are Easy; part (c) requires combining the UAM result
    with a separate constant-velocity relation — pushes the whole item to
    Intermediate.]

11. Exercise 2.27 (p.60) - BIO Mars rock ejection: constant acceleration to
    reach escape velocity 5.0 km/s over 4.0 m; find acceleration (in m/s^2
    and g's) and duration, then relate to given bacterial-survival data.
    [Core physics is Easy (two direct equations), but the added
    interpretive/comparative step with the 450,000g survival data makes
    the overall problem Intermediate.]

12. Exercise 2.30 (p.61) - Block slides down frictionless incline, released
    from rest; speed is 3.80 m/s after 6.80 m. Find speed after only 3.40 m.
    [Requires first solving for the unknown constant acceleration, then
    reapplying it to a different distance — a two-stage derivation.]

13. Problem 2.53 (p.61) - BIO Sprinter accelerates for 2.0 s to reach a
    maximum speed of 10 m/s, then runs at constant speed; find distance to
    reach max speed and average velocity over races of three different
    lengths.
    [Two-phase motion (accelerate, then constant velocity) combined for a
    total-race average velocity — matches Intermediate criteria exactly.]

14. Problem 2.57 (p.62) - Rocket clears launch platform (63 m) at 1.15 s,
    then reaches 1.00 km after an additional 4.75 s; find average velocity
    for two different sub-intervals.
    [Requires careful bookkeeping of two overlapping time/position
    intervals using only the basic average-velocity relation, vertical
    orientation aside — no free-fall/g needed since engine is still firing.]

15. Problem 2.58 (p.62) - Block moving east at 12.0 m/s decelerates west at
    2.00 m/s^2; find time it returns to x=0, and the maximum eastward
    distance reached and the time to reach it.
    [Directly extends Guided Example 1's direction-reversal idea, but
    requires solving a quadratic for two roots (Eq.2) and separately
    applying v=0 (Eq.1) to find the turning point — clearly two combined
    multi-step derivations.]

16. Problem 2.59 (p.62) - Block sliding down an incline with constant
    acceleration reaches 3.00 m/s after 8.00 m from rest; find speed and
    time to reach 16.0 m.
    [Same two-stage method as Exercise 2.30 — solve for unknown
    acceleration first, then reapply.]

17. Problem 2.60 (p.62) - Subway train: accelerates for 14.0 s, constant
    speed for 70.0 s, decelerates to stop; find total distance covered.
    [Three-phase motion problem requiring three separate distance
    calculations that are then summed — still only the four slide
    equations, but clearly multi-step.]

18. Problem 2.63 (p.63) - Ball rolls down a hill with uniform acceleration
    from rest, covering 200 m during the "second 5.0 s" of motion; find the
    distance covered during the first 5.0 s.
    [Requires the "interval subtraction" technique — using x(10s)-x(5s)=200 m
    to solve for acceleration — a clever but purely algebraic extension of
    Equation 2, no new physics concept.]

19. Problem 2.64 (p.63) - Rock moving +x at 16.0 m/s undergoes constant
    -x acceleration of 4.00 m/s^2; find the three times at which the rock is
    24.0 m from its t=0 position, and its velocity at each time.
    [Requires solving |x|=24 as two separate quadratics and interpreting
    multiple valid roots/directions — more elaborate than a single
    Guided-Example substitution but uses only Equation 2 and Equation 1.]

20. Problem 2.67 (p.63) - Sprinter runs 100 m dash in 12.0 s: accelerates
    from rest for 3.0 s, then constant speed for the remainder; find the
    acceleration.
    [Two-phase motion requiring a system of equations linking the phases
    through the common velocity at the 3.0 s mark — same style as Problem
    2.53 but solved in reverse (find acceleration, not distance).]
