REFERENCE BASELINE — WHAT THE SLIDES ACTUALLY COVER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The slide deck derives and applies FOUR kinematic equations for HORIZONTAL,
one-dimensional, constant-acceleration motion only:
  Eq. 1:  v  = v0 + at                    (no x)
  Eq. 2:  x  = x0 + v0 t + (1/2) a t^2     (no v)
  Eq. 3:  v^2 = v0^2 + 2a(x - x0)          (no t)
  Eq. 4:  x  = x0 + (1/2)(v0 + v) t        (no a)
  plus the intermediate relation  v_avg = (v0 + v) / 2  (valid ONLY for
  constant acceleration), and the basic definitions vavg = Delta-x / Delta-t
  and a = Delta-v / Delta-t used in the equations' derivations.
Four guided examples model the expected problem style: single-object,
one or two "phases" at most, direct algebraic substitution/rearrangement,
careful sign convention (e.g., negative acceleration = deceleration).
NOTABLY, two of Serway's own textbook worked Examples (Example 2.7 - the
aircraft-carrier jet landing, and Example 2.8 - a car/trooper problem) are
the SAME scenarios used as Guided Examples 3 and (implicitly, for the
two-object pursuit style) referenced in the slide deck's aircraft-carrier
example - several Serway end-of-chapter problems explicitly say "In Example
2.7..." or "Solve Example 2.8...", confirming this chapter is the direct
source of the slide's Guided Examples 1-4 (the CRT-electron and parcel-of-air
problems below are near-verbatim matches).
The slides do NOT cover: free fall / vertical motion with g, projectile
motion, two-object relative motion or pursuit problems, graphical (x-t,
v-t, a-t) analysis/sketching, motion diagrams, calculus (derivatives/
integrals) for non-constant acceleration, or 2D/constrained (rigid-body,
rod-and-rail) kinematics.

CLASSIFICATION CRITERIA USED BELOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EASY          - Single-phase, single (horizontal) object; solved by direct
                substitution into one (or a simple immediate two-step chain
                of) the four equations, or a direct Delta-x/Delta-t average-
                velocity read from data/a function - same structure/method
                as the four Guided Examples.
INTERMEDIATE  - Still only the four equations / vavg-avg-acceleration
                definitions and still horizontal/single "object system,"
                BUT requires multiple phases of motion, an intermediate
                unknown, a symbolic derivation, or a system of two
                equations - i.e., a multi-step approach not directly
                modeled by a single Guided Example.
CHALLENGING   - Requires a concept or technique NOT covered on the slides:
                free fall / vertical motion (uses g), two independently
                moving objects (relative motion, pursuit, collision),
                graph/motion-diagram reading or sketching as the core task,
                calculus/non-constant acceleration, or 2D/constrained
                (non-straight-line) kinematics.

Each entry below lists the problem number, a short description (NOT
solved), and the one-line justification for its category. Page references
are to the printed textbook page numbers appearing in the PDF footer.

EASY  (7 items)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Problem 1 (p.48, Sec. 2.1) - Estimate the time for a nerve impulse
   (~100 m/s) to travel from a stubbed toe to the brain.
   [Direct t = distance/speed order-of-magnitude estimate - same
   "subtract/divide" displacement-and-rate reasoning stressed in the slide
   6/7 notes on computing displacement and average velocity.]

2. Problem 2 (p.48, Sec. 2.1) - Particle position x = 10t^2; find average
   velocity for two different time intervals.
   [Only requires evaluating the position function at two times and
   computing Delta-x/Delta-t - the exact average-velocity definition used
   throughout the slide derivations (e.g., in deriving Equation 4), no
   calculus/instantaneous-velocity step required.]

3. Problem 3 (p.48, Sec. 2.1) - Table of x vs. t for a pinewood derby car;
   find average velocity over three different intervals.
   [Directly mirrors the slide's own introductory table (slide 5, time vs.
   velocity) - reading tabulated motion data and computing Delta-x/Delta-t.]

4. Problem 14 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - Electron in a CRT accelerates uniformly
   from 2.00x10^4 m/s to 6.00x10^6 m/s over 1.50 cm; find the time interval
   and the acceleration.
   [This is a VERBATIM match to the slide's own Guided Example 4 - identical
   numbers and scenario.]

5. Problem 15 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - Parcel of air with constant acceleration
   -4.00 m/s^2 has velocity 13.0 m/s at 10:05:00 a.m.; find velocity one
   second later, four seconds later, one second earlier, describe the
   shape of a v-t graph, and discuss whether knowing a single acceleration
   value is like knowing a whole list of velocities.
   [This is a VERBATIM match to the slide's own Guided Example 1 for parts
   (a)-(c). Parts (d)-(e) only ask the student to describe/argue using the
   same v = v0 + at relation (a straight-line v-t graph is the direct
   consequence of that equation) - no new technique or off-slide concept is
   required to answer them.]

6. Problem 16 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - Jet lands on solid ground at 100 m/s,
   maximum deceleration magnitude 5.00 m/s^2; find the minimum time to
   stop and check whether it can stop within a 0.800 km runway.
   [Directly parallels the slide's Guided Example 3 (aircraft-carrier jet
   landing) - chain v = v0 + at (solve for t) then v^2 = v0^2 + 2a(x - x0)
   (solve for distance), identical two-step method.]

7. Problem 17 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - Object with uniform acceleration has
   velocity 12.0 cm/s at x = 3.00 cm; its x-coordinate 2.00 s later is
   given; find the acceleration.
   [This is a VERBATIM match (same numbers/setup) to the slide's own
   Guided Example 2 - direct substitution into x = x0 + v0 t + (1/2) a t^2
   solved for a.]

INTERMEDIATE  (9 items)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Problem 4 (p.48, Sec. 2.2) - Swimmer's average velocity, symbolically,
   for the first half, second half, and round trip of a pool swim; also
   find average speed for the round trip.
   [Fully symbolic (no numbers), and requires recognizing that average
   speed for the round trip is total distance/total time - NOT the simple
   average of the two leg-speeds - a conceptual step beyond direct
   substitution, though it still only uses the average-velocity/speed
   definitions from the slides.]

2. Problem 6 (p.48, Sec. 2.3) - Car travels distance d at 60.0 mi/h then
   another distance d at an unknown constant speed so that the overall
   average velocity is 30.0 mi/h; find that speed, then a "What If?"
   variant with a return trip.
   [Two-segment constant-velocity algebra, same intermediate complexity as
   Young & Freedman's "Pillar to Post" style problems - requires setting
   up and solving an average-velocity equation with two unknown segment
   times.]

3. Problem 7 (p.48, Sec. 2.3) - Trip with a constant-speed driving portion
   plus a 22.0-min rest stop; given overall average speed, find total time
   and total distance.
   [Requires setting up a combined time/distance equation that accounts
   for a zero-distance rest interval - one extra reasoning layer beyond
   single-equation substitution.]

4. Problem 19 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - Glider of length l passes through a
   photogate; argue (symbolically, no numbers) whether the average velocity
   measured by the photogate equals the instantaneous velocity at the
   spatial midpoint vs. the temporal midpoint of the glider's passage.
   [This is a symbolic derivation/argument task using only the definitions
   of average and instantaneous velocity under constant acceleration - the
   same style of algebraic reasoning used in the slides' own equation
   derivations (Slides 10-17), just applied to a new symbolic scenario
   rather than a numeric Guided Example.]

5. Problem 20 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - "Why is this situation impossible?" A
   rhinoceros starts from rest, covers 50.0 m in 10.0 s with constant
   acceleration, and has a final speed of 8.00 m/s.
   [Requires computing the implied acceleration from x = (1/2)at^2 AND
   independently from v = at, then showing the two results contradict each
   other - a two-equation consistency check beyond a single Guided-Example
   substitution.]

6. Problem 21 (p.49, Sec. 2.7) - A glider passes three points along an air
   track under constant acceleration, with three measured time intervals
   between its front/back ends crossing each point; find the average speed
   at one point and the train's acceleration, and explain why the distance
   between points is not needed.
   [Requires chaining several average-velocity intervals (photogate
   crossings approximate the instantaneous velocity at each point's
   crossing time) and using differences in those velocities over the known
   time intervals to get acceleration - multi-step, but uses only the
   average-velocity/acceleration definitions from the slides, no new
   physical concept.]

7. Problem 22 (p.49-50, Sec. 2.7) - Given that the standard constant-
   acceleration equations omit x, v, a, or t in turn, derive the fifth
   equation of the set - the one that omits the initial velocity vxi.
   [This is the exact derivation METHOD demonstrated on Slides 10-17
   (algebraically eliminating one variable at a time), just applied to
   produce an additional equation not explicitly derived on the slides -
   matches the taught technique but requires independent multi-step
   symbolic work.]

8. Problem 24 (p.50, Sec. 2.7) - A decelerating car passes three evenly
   spaced roadside poles at unevenly spaced times (t=0, 10.0 s, 25.0 s);
   find the car's acceleration, its velocity at the first pole, and how
   many more poles it passes before stopping.
   [Requires setting up and solving a system of two equations (from the
   two known position/time pairs) for the two unknowns v0 and a using
   x = x0 + v0 t + (1/2) a t^2, then a further application of v^2 = v0^2 +
   2a(x-x0) to find the total stopping distance - clearly multi-step, but
   uses only the four slide equations.]

9. Problem 41 (p.51, Additional Problems) - A departing subway train's
   passing cars (each 8.60 m long) take two different measured times to
   pass a stationary observer; find the train's constant acceleration.
   [Same technique as Problem 21 - treat each car's passing time as an
   average-velocity measurement at that moment, then use the difference in
   these two velocities over the elapsed time to find acceleration -
   multi-step but no new concept beyond the slides' equations.]
